Somewhere around year eight or nine, I noticed something a bit embarrassing: the moments I was proudest of at work were almost never the clever solutions. They were the times I'd asked one question, early, that saved everyone three weeks of building the wrong thing. Nobody claps for that in the moment. It just quietly doesn't become a disaster later, which is a strange kind of achievement to feel good about — but I've come around to it.

This is an essay about that habit, written honestly rather than impressively. I've spent most of my career in telecom, banking, automotive, and asset finance — not, I should say plainly, in a trust-and-safety team or a fraud desk. So what follows isn't "here's my expertise in fraud detection." It's closer to: here's what fifteen years of building real systems in fraud-adjacent industries taught me to ask, and here's me being deliberate about turning that instinct into something sharper.

## What "senior" quietly means

Before the specifics, the pattern underneath all of them, because it's the actual point. Junior engineers and PMs — and I count several younger versions of myself in this — get very good at finding *an* answer. What takes longer to build is the instinct for which question, asked at the start, would have made the answer obvious, or made the whole feature unnecessary, or saved a very awkward conversation eight months later.

I've come to think of this less as a "T-shaped" thing — one deep skill, broad awareness everywhere else — and more as a star. The depth needs to show up in more than one place, because the interesting problems always sit where two domains meet: a product decision that's secretly a fraud decision, a scaling choice that's secretly a leadership choice. One of those five points is honestly newer and thinner than the others, and I'd rather say so than pretend otherwise.

## Fraud isn't a feeling — it's about six patterns

The word "fraud" gets used as a vague dread, and vague dread is useless in a design review. What actually helped me was learning that it breaks into a small number of concrete, learnable patterns — each with its own shape, and its own one good question.

<FraudPatternGallery />

None of these require a security certification to reason about. What they require is a habit — asking "and how would someone abuse this, specifically?" out loud, in the room, before the feature ships rather than after the postmortem. I picked this up sideways: years of banking work meant KYC and transaction monitoring were always somewhere nearby, even on projects where I wasn't the one building them; years in telecom meant SIM-swap and roaming fraud were the kind of thing that came up in a hallway conversation before an incident review. I didn't own those systems. But being near them long enough rubs off, if you let it — you start hearing the shape of the question even in rooms that have nothing to do with fraud.

## The story that's actually mine to tell

If the fraud instinct is borrowed and sharpened, this next part is the opposite — something I built and can point to directly, and it's the one I probably undersell the most.

At KPN, I helped take the developer portal from an internal incubator project to something generating real, meaningful revenue. That's not a line I say often, because it sounds like a boast the way I just wrote it — but strip the boast out and it's just a fact: audience, positioning, and a product decision that had to actually hold up against a P&L, not just a roadmap slide. The questions that mattered there were quieter than the fraud ones, but just as sharp: who is this actually for, who are we explicitly *not* building for, and what would tell us early that we'd guessed wrong. That last one — running the premortem before the postmortem is forced on you — is, I think, the single most underused habit in product work. Everyone loves a retro. Almost nobody schedules the conversation that could have prevented needing one.

## Where fraud-thinking and systems-thinking meet

There's a domain where the trust questions and the engineering questions stop being separate conversations, and it's the one I find myself most drawn to learning more about right now: safety-critical systems — the IoT and drone-adjacent end of the spectrum, where "we'll patch it in the next release" stops being an available answer.

The questions sharpen there rather than change entirely: what's the failure mode if a component goes silent mid-operation, is there a human in the loop who can actually intervene in time, and — the one that borrows straight from fraud thinking — what does the audit trail need to look like *before* a regulator asks for it, not after. I'll be honest that this is the newest point on my star, not the oldest. I'm building it the way I've built the others: by reading the people who've actually done it, and by asking the question in rooms where it's relevant, even when it's not strictly my job to ask it yet.

## The leadership underneath all of it

I want to be precise here, because overclaiming this would undercut everything I've just said about honesty. What I have is technical leadership — setting direction, unblocking people, mentoring, being the person who says "here's the call and here's why" — not a large formal team's headcount and performance reviews. I think that distinction matters, and I'd rather state it plainly than let a vaguer word do work it hasn't earned.

What I have learned, in that scope, is something the research backs up better than my own opinion would: Amy Edmondson's work on psychological safety found, across dozens of teams, that the ones who could say "I think this is wrong" out loud consistently outperformed the ones who couldn't — by a wide margin, not a rounding error. The job, as I've come to understand it, isn't to be right most often. It's to build a room where someone junior feels safe telling you you're about to be wrong — and then, when they do, to actually thank them instead of getting defensive about it, which is harder than it sounds and easier than people make it out to be, if you practise it enough times that it stops feeling personal.

## Why I'm writing this down at all

Partly for the readers — because I think "ask better questions" gets said so often as career advice that it's stopped meaning anything, and I wanted to write down the actual fifteen questions rather than the slogan. But mostly, if I'm honest, for myself. Writing this forced me to separate what I've genuinely earned from what I'm still building — the fraud instinct that's real but adjacent, the leadership that's real but scoped, the safety-critical thinking that's real but new. I'd rather know exactly where those lines are than blur them, because the moment you blur them in an interview, or in your own head, is usually the moment you stop actually closing the gap.

The honest version of a strong profile isn't one with no gaps. It's one that can tell you, calmly and specifically, exactly where the gaps are — and what it's doing about each one. That's the whole essay, really. I just needed fifteen questions and a slightly too-long walk through KPN's developer portal to say it properly.
