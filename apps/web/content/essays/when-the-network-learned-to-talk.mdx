Let me confess something about my own field: telecom sounds boring. It's the plumbing behind the plumbing — the thing the *interesting* people build on top of. For most of my career that was a fair description.

It isn't anymore. The plumbing is waking up. And almost nobody outside the industry has noticed, which is exactly when the boring thing quietly becomes magic.

Here's the whole idea in one breath: **for a hundred years the phone network was a dumb, reliable pipe. Now it's becoming a smart, programmable one — something your apps, and increasingly AI, can actually talk to and give instructions.** That's it. Everything else is detail.

## Why you should care (some numbers)

When something *this* big changes shape, it's worth a look. Reports from the GSMA — the industry's own body — put it at roughly **5.5 billion people** on mobile and **8-billion-plus connections**, a system that accounts for something like **5% of global GDP**. On top of that sit an estimated **15 billion connected "things"** — sensors, trackers, meters — heading toward **30 billion by 2030**.

So this isn't a niche. It's the nervous system of the modern world, and it's mid-upgrade.

## The story in three acts

**Act one — the closed pipe.** For decades the network was a marvel of reliability and a fortress of closedness. A call from Amsterdam found a phone in Mumbai in seconds — genuinely miraculous engineering — but you could only *use* it, never build on it. And its trust ran on the honour system: the old signalling underneath simply believed whatever another operator told it. Which is why, famously, security researchers once intercepted a sitting US congressman's calls and texts on live television, just by abusing that trust. Hold onto that, because it's the pattern of the whole story: **the network's greatest strength and its deepest flaw kept turning out to be the same thing.** Reliable because rigid. Universal because trusting.

**Act two — the programmable network.** Then telecom did something it had never done: it handed out keys. First the small magic — sending a text became a single line of code (I still find that a little wondrous: a snippet runs, and a device in someone's pocket buzzes). Then the big shift, happening right now — the network's *deepest* powers opening up as ordinary APIs. Where a device really is. Whether its SIM was swapped an hour ago. A guaranteed-fast connection for the next ten minutes. Things that used to need a six-month telco integration are becoming a function call.

**Act three — the intelligent network.** Now add AI, and the arrows point both ways. AI *for* the network (spotting fraud, predicting where it'll buckle, eventually healing itself), and — the part I find genuinely thrilling — the network *for* AI.

## My slightly opinionated bet

Here's where I'll stick my neck out. I think the network becomes a **sense organ for AI** — a source of trustworthy, real-time facts that a model can't get anywhere else. Where you are (verified by the network, not a spoofable GPS chip). Whether your identity just changed. How good your connection is, right now. Give an AI agent those senses and it stops guessing about the physical world and starts *knowing*.

That's the optimistic case, and I mostly believe it. But every act of this story trades a little safety for a lot of reach — the closed pipe became open, the open network is becoming intelligent, and each step is a gift and a liability wearing the same coat. The gifts are worth chasing. The liabilities are worth naming out loud.
